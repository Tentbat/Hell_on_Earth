# Lightning and storms from above

## What you see

From cruise altitude a thunderstorm is a tower of cloud rising from near the ground to at or above your height, topped by a flat anvil that trails downwind. The top can overshoot in a bulging dome where the strongest updraft punches into the stratosphere. At night the whole tower glows from inside with each flash, and because you are above most of the haze you can see storms 200 miles away.

## How far is it?

If you see a flash and hear nothing, that is normal: thunder never reaches the cabin. Judge distance by size instead. An anvil that fills a hand-width of your window at arm's length is roughly 50 miles away. Pilots keep at least 20 miles from a strong storm, so anything close enough to show texture is being avoided, not approached.

## Why planes avoid them

The danger is not the lightning. It is the air: updrafts and downdrafts of 50 miles an hour or more, hail that can dent an aircraft and ice that forms in minutes. The radar in the nose shows the pilots where the heavy rain is, and they route around it. The bumps you may feel are the edges of the storm's outflow, not the storm itself.

## When the plane is struck

Commercial aircraft are struck about once a year each. The current flows over the aluminium or the conductive mesh in the composite skin and leaves through the wingtips or tail. Passengers may see a flash and hear a bang. The aircraft is designed for it and is inspected afterwards.

## Sprites and jets

Above big storms, far above the anvil, there are flashes almost nobody sees: red sprites that look like jellyfish 50 miles up, and blue jets shooting from the top of the cloud. Astronauts photograph them. From a window seat on a dark night, with a storm complex far off to one side, you have a real if small chance of catching a sprite as a brief red flicker above the lightning. Keep the cabin lights off your window.

## Storms you can predict

Thunderstorms over land peak in the late afternoon and evening, when the ground has heated all day. Over the sea they peak before dawn. On a summer evening across the Great Plains or the Southeast, a window on the shaded side of the aircraft is a front-row seat.

## Squall lines and supercells

A line of storms hundreds of miles long is a squall line, usually along a cold front. A single rotating storm with a sloping, tilted tower and an overshooting dome is a supercell, the kind that produces tornadoes. From above you may see a flanking line of smaller towers feeding into the main one, like steps up to a throne.
