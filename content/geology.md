# Reading the geology

## Volcanoes

A lone cone with snow on top, standing above everything else, is a stratovolcano like Rainier or Shasta. A broad, gently sloping dome is a shield volcano. Black, treeless patches on otherwise vegetated land are young lava flows; the younger, the blacker. Small conical hills with a dimple on top are cinder cones. A round lake with steep sides and no inlet is a crater or caldera lake.

## Faults

Straight-edged mountain fronts, like the east face of the Sierra Nevada or the Tetons, are fault scarps: the mountains went up, the valley went down. Streams that jog sideways all along a line mark a strike-slip fault like the San Andreas, where the two sides slide past each other. Long straight valleys through hills often follow faults too.

## Folds

Where rock layers have been bent, erosion cuts them into long parallel ridges and valleys, like the Appalachians. Tilted layers appear as stripes of different colour running across the country. A dome shows as concentric rings; the Black Hills are a huge one.

## Glaciers and what they leave behind

Ice carves U-shaped valleys with flat floors and steep walls; rivers carve V-shapes. Long narrow lakes in parallel valleys (the Finger Lakes, Lake Chelan) were gouged by ice. Rounded knobs and bowl-shaped hollows high on mountains are cirques, where glaciers began. Thousands of small lakes scattered randomly across flat country, as in Minnesota and northern Canada, are kettle lakes left by melting ice. Long low ridges across the plains are moraines.

## Ice-age floods

Eastern Washington's Channeled Scablands are a landscape torn by floods when a glacial dam broke repeatedly: dry waterfalls, braided channels cut in bare basalt, and giant ripple marks visible only from the air.

## Rivers

A braided river carries more sediment than it can hold and spreads into many channels. A meandering river loops across a flat floodplain, and its abandoned loops survive as oxbow lakes. Where a river leaves mountains onto a plain it dumps its load as an alluvial fan, a smooth cone with the river splitting across it. Where it meets the sea it builds a delta.

## Deserts

Sand dunes come in shapes set by the wind: crescents (barchans) where sand is scarce and wind steady, long ridges (linear dunes) where winds shift between two directions, and star dunes where they come from everywhere. Dry lake beds (playas) are flat, pale and often salt-crusted. Dark varnish on old gravel surfaces makes desert plains look brown-black.

## Salt and evaporites

Salt flats are the brightest natural surfaces on Earth. Salt domes push up through sediments as round hills along the Gulf coast. Salt lakes turn pink and red as they concentrate.

## Karst

Limestone country dissolves. From above it shows as countless round sinkholes, often filled with small circular lakes, in Florida, Kentucky and the Yucatan. Rivers appear and disappear.

## Impact craters

Rare and precious. Meteor Crater in Arizona is the textbook: a neat round bowl with a raised rim. Older craters have been eroded or filled and show only as circular lakes, ring-shaped ridges or a round patch of different vegetation.

## Reading the colours

- Red and orange: iron-stained sandstone, the Colorado Plateau.
- White: salt, gypsum, limestone, snow.
- Black: young lava, coal, shale.
- Green: vegetation, so also water.
- Turquoise: glacial meltwater lakes full of rock flour, or mine tailings.
