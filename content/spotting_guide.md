# How to spot things from the air

Most people look out of an aeroplane window and see "land". With a few habits you will see a great deal more.

## Know where the horizon is

At cruise altitude, around 35,000 feet, the horizon is about 230 miles away. Anything you can see is somewhere between the ground directly below and that line. Features near the horizon are tens of miles away and look flat and hazy; features in the lower half of the window are within about 20 miles and show real detail.

A useful rule: the angle below the horizon tells you the distance. Halfway between the horizon and straight down (45 degrees) is about 7 miles out at cruise. A quarter of the way down from the horizon (about 22 degrees) is roughly 17 miles out. Most of what the app points out sits in that band.

## Haze hides contrast, not size

Dust, smoke and humidity scatter light, so distant objects lose contrast long before they lose size. Dark things on pale ground (lava flows, forests on desert, rivers in farmland) survive haze best. Pale things on pale ground (a town in a dry valley) vanish first. Look for edges and shadows rather than colours.

## Use the sun

Low sun is the spotter's friend. In the hour after sunrise and before sunset, every ridge, dune and river bluff throws a shadow and the land turns three-dimensional. At midday the same terrain looks flat. If you have a choice of seats, the side away from the sun gives clearer views with less glare, but the side toward the sun shows water surfaces as bright mirrors, which makes lakes and rivers easy to find.

## Let your eyes adapt and cup the glare

Cabin lights and a bright screen make the outside look dim. Cup your hand against the window for ten seconds and the view brightens noticeably. Turn your screen brightness down.

## Natural or made by people?

- Straight lines and right angles are almost always human: roads, field edges, canals, railway cuttings, borders.
- Perfect circles are human too: irrigation pivots, storage tanks, cooling towers. Nature draws circles only as craters, cinder cones and a few sinkhole lakes.
- Nature draws curves that branch: rivers, deltas, dune crests, glacial valleys.
- Geometric patches of a single colour are farms. Mottled patches are forest, scrub or grazing land.

## Reservoir or natural lake?

A reservoir has a straight edge somewhere (the dam), a long branching shape that follows the old river valleys, and often a pale "bathtub ring" along the shore where the water has dropped. A natural lake sits in a basin with rounded shores and rarely has a straight edge.

## Braided or meandering river?

A braided river splits into many shallow channels around gravel bars and looks like frayed rope. It carries a heavy sediment load, typically from mountains or glaciers upstream. A meandering river is a single channel in big loops, with abandoned loops nearby as crescent-shaped oxbow lakes. It flows across a flat plain with fine sediment.

## Wind farm or solar farm?

Wind turbines are tiny white specks in neat lines along ridges or in grids across farmland, each with a thin shadow. Solar farms are dark rectangles that can flash bright when they catch the sun at the right angle, usually on flat ground near a road and a power line.

## Contrails

A fresh contrail is sharp and white directly behind an aircraft. Over minutes it spreads and softens. In humid air at altitude it persists for hours and can spread into a thin cloud sheet. In dry air it vanishes within a wingspan or two. Count the contrails: busy air corridors show them in parallel lines.

## Sunrise and sunset from above

You see the sun earlier at sunrise and later at sunset than people on the ground, because you are above the horizon they see. The dark band that climbs the eastern sky after sunset is the shadow of the Earth itself, with a pink band (the Belt of Venus) above it.
