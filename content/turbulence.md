# Why is it bumpy?

Turbulence is air moving in different directions at different speeds. The aircraft flies through the edges between them and gets pushed around. It is uncomfortable, it is rarely dangerous, and it almost always has an identifiable cause.

## Low-level bumps on climb and descent

Below about 10,000 feet the air is stirred by the ground: warm patches rise, hills deflect the wind, sea breezes push inland. On a hot afternoon over land the climb is bumpy; in the cool of the morning it is smooth. This kind fades as you climb.

## Convection

Thunderstorms and even large fair-weather cumulus are columns of rising air surrounded by sinking air. Flying between or over them means crossing those boundaries. Pilots avoid the storms themselves; the ride near them can still be rough.

## Jet stream and clear-air turbulence

The jet stream is a river of wind at cruise altitude, 100 to 250 miles an hour, a few hundred miles wide. Where it meets slower air, especially on its northern edge and where it bends, the wind changes speed sharply over a short distance (wind shear) and the air breaks into eddies. There is no cloud to show it, which is why the seatbelt sign can come on in clear blue sky. This is the most common turbulence at cruise, and it is forecast reasonably well.

## Mountain wave

Wind flowing over a mountain range sets up waves downwind, like water over a boulder in a stream. The waves can extend 100 miles from the mountains and far above their tops. Smooth lens-shaped (lenticular) clouds mark the crests, but the waves are there without them. Crossing the Rockies, the Sierra or the Cascades with a strong west wind is the classic case. The bumps come in slow heaves rather than sharp jolts, sometimes with a rotor of rougher air low down on the lee side.

## Wake turbulence

A brief sharp jolt with no other cause may be the wake of another aircraft that passed ahead of you minutes earlier. Big aircraft leave two counter-rotating vortices that sink slowly and drift with the wind.

## Why the seatbelt sign

Pilots turn it on when they expect bumps, from the forecast, from reports by aircraft ahead, or from what they feel. Injuries in turbulence almost all happen to people not wearing a belt. The sign is prediction, not alarm.

## Is it dangerous?

Modern airliners are tested to loads far beyond anything turbulence produces. The wing of a 787 is bent upward by more than 7 metres in testing without breaking. Severe turbulence, where the aircraft is momentarily out of control and loose objects fly, is rare and lasts seconds. The real risk is to unbelted passengers and crew, and to hot drinks.

## What the app does with this

The ride outlook on the Weather screen is built from forecast winds aloft (wind shear marks the jet stream), terrain under the route (mountain wave) and, when connected, pilot reports and official turbulence guidance. Treat it as "where bumps are plausible", not a schedule.
