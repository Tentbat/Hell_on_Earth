# Sky tricks: glories, halos, contrails and more

## The glory

Look at the cloud deck below on the side away from the sun. Around the aircraft's shadow you may see a small ring of rainbow colours, red outside and blue inside: a glory. It is made by sunlight scattered back toward you from tiny water droplets, and it is centred exactly on the point opposite the sun, which is where your shadow is. Everyone on that side of the aircraft sees their own glory centred on their own line of sight.

## Sub-sun and sub-parhelia

When flying above a layer of ice-crystal cloud with the sun low, you may see a bright blob of light on the cloud below, where the sun's reflection would be if the cloud were a mirror: a sub-sun. Flat ice crystals floating level act as millions of tiny mirrors. Coloured spots either side of it are sub-parhelia.

## Halos

A ring around the sun at 22 degrees, about a hand-span at arm's length, is caused by ice crystals in cirrus above you. From cruise altitude you can be inside the cirrus, and the halo becomes vivid.

## Earth's shadow and the Belt of Venus

After sunset, look east. A dark blue-grey band rises from the horizon: that is the shadow of the Earth cast onto the atmosphere. Above it is a band of pink, the Belt of Venus, where the high air is still lit by the reddened sun. From the air you see both far more clearly than from the ground.

## Contrails

An engine exhaust contains water vapour; at cruise temperatures of minus 40 to minus 60 C it freezes into ice crystals within a wingspan behind the engine. In dry air the crystals evaporate in seconds and the trail is short. In humid air they persist and spread, sometimes into a cirrus sheet that covers the sky. Shadows of contrails on the cloud below, and the dark "distrail" where a contrail has cleared a lane through a thin cloud layer, are both worth watching for.

## Crepuscular and anticrepuscular rays

Beams of light and shadow fanning out from the sun behind clouds are crepuscular rays. Look to the opposite horizon and you may see them converging again: anticrepuscular rays. They are parallel beams that only look fan-shaped because of perspective.

## Aurora

On night flights at high latitudes, over Canada, Alaska or the North Atlantic, the northern lights appear as green curtains and arcs, sometimes with red tops, on the side of the aircraft facing the pole. Switch off your reading light and cup your hands to the window. The app's aurora alert uses the Kp index when it has a connection.

## The Moon and planets

From cruise altitude the sky is darker and the air thinner, so Venus and Jupiter blaze and the Moon shows its craters to the naked eye along the shadow line. Earthshine, the faint glow of the Moon's dark side lit by the Earth, is easy to see from a dark cabin.

## Night lights

City lights trace the shape of settlement: a grid, a river, a coastline. Orange sodium lamps are being replaced by white LEDs, so older districts glow orange and newer ones blue-white. Isolated bright points in empty country are gas flares at oil fields, greenhouses, or prisons. A line of red lights slowly blinking in unison is a wind farm.
