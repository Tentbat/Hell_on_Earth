# Clouds from above

From an airliner you see clouds from a side almost nobody else does: the top. Here is what the main types look like from 35,000 feet and what they tell you about the ride.

## Cumulus (fair-weather puffs)

Small white cauliflower clouds with flat bases all at the same height, dotted across the land below. Each one sits on top of a column of rising warm air. They form over land on sunny afternoons and fade at dusk. They cast neat dark shadows, and from above the pattern often follows the ground: more cloud over dark fields and forest, less over lakes and cool coasts.

## Stratocumulus (the lumpy blanket)

A sheet of grey-white lumps with gaps between them, very common over the ocean and over land in the morning. It often ends at a sharp edge where the air changes, for example at a coastline. Flying over a stratocumulus deck feels like crossing a cobbled road made of cotton wool.

## Stratus and fog

A smooth featureless sheet filling valleys and lowlands, with hills poking through as islands. Winter fog in the Central Valley of California or the Swiss Plateau looks exactly like this. The tops are flat because they are capped by warmer air above.

## Cumulonimbus (thunderstorms)

The giants. A single storm tower can rise from a base near the ground to above your cruising altitude, with a flat anvil top spreading downwind and a hard, boiling cauliflower crown. Pilots fly around them, never through. The anvil can stretch for 100 miles. At night you may see lightning flicker inside one from a great distance.

## Cirrus (the high wisps)

Thin streaks and hooks of ice crystals at or above your altitude. If you can see them above you, they are very high indeed, around 40,000 feet. Cirrus arriving from the west often precedes a weather front by a day.

## Cloud streets

Long parallel lines of small cumulus, all aligned with the wind. The air rolls in horizontal tubes, like rollers under a carpet, and cloud forms along the rising edges. Common over the Great Plains and over cold water downwind of warm land.

## Lenticular clouds

Smooth, lens-shaped clouds standing still downwind of mountains while the wind blows through them. They mark mountain waves in the air, which can shake an aircraft even in clear sky. If you see them ahead, expect a few bumps.

## Pileus and fumulus

A pileus is a little smooth cap riding on top of a fast-growing storm cloud, formed as the updraft pushes moist air up and over. A fumulus is a cloud formed by human heat and moisture, over a power station cooling tower, a steel works or a wildfire.

## Marine stratocumulus and the open-cell pattern

Over cool oceans, enormous sheets of cloud form honeycomb patterns. Closed cells have cloud in the middle and clear edges; open cells have clear middles and cloudy walls. The pattern flips depending on whether the air is sinking or rising in the middle of each cell.

## What the clouds say about the ride

- Below you and lumpy: smooth for you, bumpy for whoever is in them.
- Towering storms ahead: expect a detour and perhaps some bumps near the edges.
- Lenticulars or clear sky over mountains: possible mountain-wave turbulence.
- Nothing at all and a jet stream overhead: clear-air turbulence is still possible, which is why the seatbelt sign can come on in blue sky.
